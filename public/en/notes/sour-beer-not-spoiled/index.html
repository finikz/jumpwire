Sour Beer Is Not Necessarily Spoiled

Acidity can be intentional—or an unwanted fault. The whole beer matters.

JUMPWIRESeptember 14, 20263 min read

The first time someone tries a sour beer, they may ask, “Has this gone bad?” Someone else may ask why it is so refreshing. Both reactions are understandable. Some styles are intentionally tart, but not every sour, vinegary or unusual aroma deserves to be called “wild complexity.”

Acidity can come from more than one process

Sour beers may use lactic-acid bacteria, yeast or other managed fermentation methods. Lambic, gueuze, Berliner Weisse and gose can all be tart, but their traditions, ingredients and sensory profiles differ. Gose is often associated with wheat, salt and coriander; traditional lambic has a spontaneous-fermentation tradition linked to a specific area of Belgium. A style name is a clue, not a universal acidity scale.

“Normal sourness” does not mean you have to like it

Some sour beers are bright and fruity; others show more complex fermentation, wood or ageing notes. It is fine not to enjoy acidity. Ask how tart it is, whether it has fruit and whether it was barrel-aged before deciding to try it.

Do not diagnose an abnormal product from one adjective

Sensory descriptions have to be considered alongside style, storage, packaging and professional judgement. If a package is damaged, the seal looks wrong or the aroma clearly contradicts the product description, stop drinking and contact the seller. A web article cannot determine whether a particular bottle is safe, and your nose should not have to diagnose spoilage alone.

Takeaway

A sour style deserves a clear explanation. Complexity is not the same as “the stranger, the better,” and you never have to force yourself to like it.

Lambic tasting examples in “The Song of Malt: A Brief History of Beer” discuss acidity, wood and ageing as style clues—not as a template for every sour product.
