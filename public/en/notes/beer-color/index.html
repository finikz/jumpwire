What Does Beer Colour Tell You?

Colour is a clue, not an identity card.

JUMPWIRESeptember 14, 20263 min read

Gold, amber, brown and deep black can feel like filters that write the ending before you take a sip. Colour is more like a trailer: it offers a clue, but it cannot reveal alcohol strength, bitterness or quality.

Where does beer colour come from?

Malt is a major source of colour. How malt is kilned or roasted, and how much is used, can shape the beer’s appearance. The boil, recipe, brewing method and filtration can matter too, so no single colour formula explains every beer.

What might gold, amber and dark colours suggest?

Gold is common in many lagers, wheat beers and pale ales. A beer may be crisp, malty, fruity or full-bodied; colour cannot settle the question. Amber and copper shades appear in some amber and red ales, but hops, fermentation and body still depend on the beer itself.

Dark malts may suggest roast, cocoa or coffee-like notes, and stouts and porters can be wonderfully varied. Not every dark beer has the same aromas, and dark does not automatically mean stronger or heavier.

How to read the glass more reliably

SRM is one system used to describe beer colour. It measures colour; it is not a quality score or a flavour label. Check the beer name and style first, then look for aroma, bitterness and ABV. Taste the beer and compare it with your own expectations. Colour can set an expectation; flavour is what you can actually describe.

Takeaway

Your eyes meet the colour first. Your palate fills in the story.

BJCP style guidance and malt references describe colour as an observation alongside, rather than a substitute for, flavour.
